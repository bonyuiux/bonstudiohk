Gallery pages and polish

Your new copy is in, and the line rules are written into the project. Two choices are left: how the photos and text sit together on the inner pages, and which finishing touches to add.

What I took from Kengo Hino's site, which every layout below now follows:

  • The gallery is one defined screen, as tall as the browser window.
  • Every photo is centred in that screen, landscape or portrait, so a portrait never leaves a gap on one side.
  • All thumbnails are the same 3:2 crop in a tight column. The current one stays in the middle, inside the camera-frame corners.

Each layout preview is a real 1280 × 800 desktop page, scaled down. Click the photo or the thumbnails to try landscape and portrait. Scroll inside a preview to see the whole page.

1. Gallery page layout

Shown on the Hong Kong page, which has the most text. Portrait, Wedding and Event get the same layout with less text.

A · Text, then the screenThe text reads first. Scroll down and the gallery fills one full window, just like Kengo Hino. The clearest split between reading and looking.I'll build this one unless you choose another
B · Side by sideThe text sits in a narrow column on the left and the gallery fills the right, both in view at once. Calm, and works best on wide screens. On phones it stacks like A.
C · Photo first, text as captionThe page opens on the photos. The title and text follow underneath, like a label beside a print. Strongest for Portrait, Wedding and Event, weaker for Hong Kong, where the session info matters.
D · Contact sheetText, then every photo as an even 3:2 grid, like a film contact sheet. Click any frame to view it full screen. Shows the whole set at a glance, but each photo is cropped until opened.

2. How the gallery area is marked

This is what stops the gallery looking sunk into the page. Only for layouts A, B and C.

A · NothingOnly the photo marks the area, as on Kengo Hino.
B · Viewfinder cornersFour thin corners mark the whole screen. Same line language as the thumbnails.My pick
C · Soft panelA slightly darker paper panel sits behind the photo, like a mount board.
